Trail Camera Live Feed: What It Takes at a Hog Trap

A trail camera live feed is only as good as the cell signal and the power at your trap. Check the signal right where the camera will hang, and plan on a solar panel and a monthly data plan before you buy anything. Get those right and you can watch the sounder come in from your phone and close the gate when the last hog is inside.

Check the signal where the camera hangs

Bars at the truck or the house don't mean bars at the trap. Take the HogEye Mini Camera to the spot where it will hang and power it up there. Once it's ready, the LOW, MED, and HIGH lights on the bottom tell you the signal at that exact spot.

If it reads low, try a better antenna before you give up on the site. The camera ships with a paddle antenna, and omni-directional and directional antenna kits are available for weak spots.

Hang it about a foot from the trap, above it and looking slightly down. From there the 140-degree view takes in the hogs inside the trap and the ones still outside, in 720p at 10 frames per second. Hang it farther back and you can miss notifications.

Plan the power and the data

Streaming takes power, so the camera runs on a 50Ah lithium battery that the included solar panel keeps charged. Face the panel south and keep it out of the shade; it needs at least 3 hours of sun a day. With direct sun on the panel, the battery lasts several months. Without the panel, a full charge lasts 5 to 6 days on average.

Then there's the monthly cost. On AT&T or Verizon, the HogEye Mini Camera is $1,299 and the data plan is $49.99 a month, with no contract. You can start or stop the plan anytime, so you aren't paying for the months the trap sits idle.

If there's no signal at all

Some trap sites won't get a usable signal no matter where you hang the camera. For those, there's a Starlink version of the HogEye Mini Camera for $2,999. Its data runs $100 a month through Wildlife Dominion, and you don't need your own Starlink account.

The camera setup guides walk through the mount and the install once you've picked your spot.

Satellite Trail Camera for Hog Traps With No Cell Signal

If your trap sits where your phone shows no bars, a cellular camera can't reach you either: no live video, and no way to send the close command. You need a satellite trail camera, and on a hog trap that means one that can close the gate too. The Starlink version of the HogEye Mini Camera connects through a Starlink Mini receiver, so you can still watch the trap and close it from home.

What comes in the Starlink kit

It's the same HogEye Mini Camera and the same HogEye app. The only change is how it gets online: over Wi-Fi from a Starlink Mini receiver instead of a cell connection. As long as the receiver can see open sky, it works almost anywhere.

The Starlink kit is $2,999, and it brings its own freestanding steel tripod, so you don't need a T-post. The receiver sits on top, the camera mounts on the side, and the included gate cable and latch adapter run from the camera to your trap's gate.

Power is in the box too: a 12V 100Ah lithium battery and a 300W foldable solar panel, sized to run the camera and the receiver together. Data and connectivity run $100 a month through Wildlife Dominion, and there's no separate Starlink account or service to buy. The HogEye setup resources cover the Starlink install.

Watching and closing over Starlink

From there it works just like the cellular version. Open the app at home and watch live, day or night, using digital pan, tilt, and zoom to check every corner. Count the hogs inside and the ones still hanging back outside. When the whole sounder is in, send the close command from your phone and the gate closes.

That's what the camera saves you on a remote trap: no drive out to find it empty, and no closing on half the sounder, which teaches the hogs that got away to avoid the trap. Anyone you add in the app can watch too, with no fee for extra users.

HogEye vs. Spartan GoLive for Hog Traps

If you’re considering a Spartan GoLive for a hog trap, check whether you can close the gate from the same screen you use to watch live video before you put any camera on the trap. Seeing the whole sounder is only useful if you can act when the last hog walks in.

What the Spartan GoLive does

The Spartan GoLive 3 is a cellular scouting camera that can send photos and video to your phone and live stream. It sends 8MP photos and 60-second HD video with sound. The GoLive 3 is listed at $289.95 to $299.95 depending on camo, and works on Verizon or AT&T. It has a 96-degree field of view, motion detection out to 80 feet, no-glow infrared to 60 feet, and a 12V jack for solar or external power. The GoLive 2 live streams at up to 30 frames per second.

That makes a GoLive useful for scouting where hogs are feeding before you set a trap. You can check activity around fresh rooting, trails, and wallows without making a trip out to the camera. But Spartan’s GoLive listings say nothing about connecting the camera to a trap gate or latch.

What your hog trap needs

At a trap, you need to see the hogs inside and outside the gate. If you close on part of the sounder, the hogs that get away can learn to avoid the trap. Live video alone does not complete that job: the camera has to connect to the gate.

The HogEye Mini Camera comes with a gate cable and latch adapter that connect it to your trap’s gate. Its live video is 720p at 10 frames per second, with a 140-degree field of view wide enough to see inside and outside the trap. In the app, watch the hogs as they enter, count the ones inside and outside, and send the close command when the whole sounder is inside. The gate closes.

The HogEye Mini Camera is $1,299 on AT&T or Verizon. Its data plan is $49.99 a month with no contract, and you can start or stop it anytime. See the HogEye Mini Camera if you want a camera that connects to the gate and lets you close it from the app.

Are Hogs Nocturnal? When the Sounder Comes to Your Trap

Hogs usually come to your trap after dark, and a pressured sounder may show up later as it learns to avoid people. Watch your trap live for a few nights, count the hogs as they come in, and use that pattern to be ready to close when the whole sounder is inside, rather than waiting to check the trap in the morning.

When hogs come in

If you’re asking, “are hogs nocturnal?”, the practical answer is that their activity can shift with the season and the pressure they’ve had. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension says feral hogs are routinely nocturnal during summer heat. In winter, they may become more active during the day as they search for food. Hunting pressure can make a sounder rarely seen in daylight in any season.

Use what you see around your trap to help make sense of the timing. Fresh rooting, trails, wallows, and rubs show where hogs have been moving, but they don’t tell you exactly when the sounder will enter. If they’re coming later after pressure, keep watching for the new pattern instead of relying on an earlier arrival time.

Count the sounder before you close

Don’t assume the whole sounder will arrive at once. Some hogs may enter while others are still outside, and closing on only part of the sounder teaches the hogs that get away to avoid the trap.

The HogEye Mini Camera streams live video to the HogEye app day or night. Use the digital pan, tilt, and zoom to watch hogs inside and outside the trap. You can also review recordings stored on the camera for 14 days to check what time hogs came in on previous nights. For help comparing camera options, see the best camera for hog trapping.

Be ready at their hour

Once you know their hour, you don’t have to watch the screen all night. The camera sends a motion alert to your phone when hogs come into the trap. Open the live view, count until you reach the number you saw on earlier nights, then send the close command from your phone. The gate closes, and there’s no morning drive to find out whether the trap is empty.

If the arrival time changes, keep using the live view and recordings to adjust when you’re ready to close.
