# How to Run a Multi-Trap Hog Operation Across a Large Property

One trap behind the barn can catch hogs. It rarely **removes a sounder** across a 2,000-acre lease when three separate sounders are rooting in different corners.

When damage spans multiple fields, you need **more than one monitored trap site**, a map that shows where each sounder travels, and a log that tells you whether to hold, move, or rest a site after each closure. That is what a multi-trap hog operation is: mixed trap types, shared monitoring, and catch records that tell you where to move next.

Bait timelines and trap-type picks are in our [baiting guide](https://hogeyecameras.com/hog-trap-baiting-guide/) and [trap comparison guide](https://hogeyecameras.com/hog-trap-comparison-corral-box-drop-net/) when you need that detail. For network-level architecture (maps, permissions, weak cell SOPs), see our [smart ranch network guide](https://hogeyecameras.com/smart-ranch-network-how-to-build-a-multi-trap-system/) as a companion resource.

## When One Trap Is Not Enough

Add a second or third monitored trap site when you see:

- **Separate sounders** on different corners of the property, not one group rotating between two feeders
- **Re-invasion** after a single catch because remaining hogs relocated to another field
- **Seasonal crop damage** moving between pastures or creek bottoms
- **Lease deadlines** (planting, hay cut, calving) that require faster reduction than one site can deliver

One corral on the wrong travel lane wastes conditioning time and may educate the sounder that interacts with it. It does not automatically train every other sounder on the property, but it does burn bait and labor on the wrong corridor while damage continues elsewhere.

Before you add traps, confirm you are dealing with **multiple sounders**, not one sounder with two feed sources. Camera time on fresh rooting clusters helps you tell the difference.

## Mapping Sounders and Trap Zones

Start with an aerial map (onX, Google Earth, or a printed property map). Mark:

- Fresh rooting clusters from the last 7–14 days
- Water and feeder locations
- Vehicle routes for bait resupply that do not cross active bait lanes at night
- No-trap zones (calving pastures, public roads, neighbor boundaries)

Assign **Zone A, B, C** labels to separate sounder corridors. Each zone gets its own trap type decision using the [trap comparison guide](https://hogeyecameras.com/hog-trap-comparison-corral-box-drop-net/).

Mapping cuts wasted drives. Instead of checking every trap on a fixed loop, you drive to the zone with **motion alerts and unclear head count**, skip quiet sites until fresh rooting returns, and keep closure windows tight where hogs are active. That is how one operator covers more acreage without guessing which pasture needs attention tonight.

Our [placement guide](https://hogeyecameras.com/hog-trap-placement-guide/) applies per zone before bait goes down.

## Trap Types Across the Same Property

Large programs often mix:

- **Corral** on high-volume sounder trails where whole-group capture is the goal
- **Box traps** for edge singles near barns or pens
- **Net systems** on open pastures where panels are impractical

Match type to **sounder size and terrain**, not uniform branding. A box trap in Zone A and a corral in Zone B is normal. Two corrals 400 yards apart on the same sounder trail is usually wasted bait.

## Scheduling Checks vs Remote Monitoring

Without cameras, multi-trap programs die from **drive time**. Operators skip distant zones when fuel and labor stack up, which means distant sounders keep breeding while the easy site behind the barn gets all the attention.

With cellular monitoring:

- Prioritize drives to sites with **motion alerts** and unclear counts
- Skip empty sites until fresh rooting or trails return
- Let one operator cover more acreage without guessing

Define **crew roles** before closure season:

- **Primary watcher** monitors live feeds during peak movement windows
- **Backup watcher** covers weak cell sites or when the primary is unavailable
- **Trigger authority** is explicit (who may send the closure command)
- **Post-closure response** (dispatch, transport, bait reset) is assigned before hogs are on site

Multi-user permissions prevent double commands and give you an audit trail when something goes wrong at 2 a.m.

## Logging Catches and Adjusting Strategy

Track for each zone:

- Date and trap type
- Estimated sounder count removed
- Bait days before capture
- Closure method (remote vs manual)
- Partial vs full capture (note if matriarch was inside)
- Check for fresh rooting or trails within 72 hours

If Zone B keeps producing partial captures, read [wild hog behavior](https://hogeyecameras.com/wild-hog-behavior-trap-timing/) and [baiting timeline](https://hogeyecameras.com/hog-trap-baiting-guide/) before moving the trap only 50 yards. Partial captures in multi-trap programs are especially costly because they can make the next zone harder while you still have active damage in the first field.

Use logs to decide:

- **Hold** the site (fresh sign, sounder reforming)
- **Move** the trap (stale sign, wrong corridor)
- **Rest** the site (trap-shy pressure, switch to another zone for two weeks)

## Coordinating Cameras, Traps, and Operators

A coordinated multi-trap program links:

- Traps and cameras by zone on one map
- Shared bait inventory and service schedule
- Trigger SOPs for weak cell sites
- Annual hardware audit (batteries, actuators, panel pins)

**Scale monitoring before you scale trap count.** Three monitored traps often outperform ten unmonitored traps because the operator can act when the full sounder is inside, not after the window is gone. Raw trap count without video and trigger discipline usually means more empty drives and more partial captures.

Texas operators should align the program with [Texas trapping rules](https://hogeyecameras.com/trapping-wild-hogs-texas/).

## FAQ

**How many hog traps do I need on a large property?**  
Start with one monitored trap site per **separate sounder corridor** with fresh rooting or trails, not one trap total for the whole acreage.

**How far apart should traps be?**  
Far enough that catching one sounder does not empty bait at another site the same night. Distance varies by density; use your map and camera logs, not a fixed yard number.

**Should every trap be electronic?**  
Prioritize remote trigger on corral and net sites where whole-sounder closure matters most. Box traps for singles may need camera monitoring only.

**How do I know if I have one sounder or three?**  
Compare rooting clusters, trail width, group size on camera, and timing. Separate sounders often hit different zones on different nights. One sounder rotates between two feed sources on a connected trail system.

**What if only one zone is producing catches?**  
Log it, then shift effort to zones with fresh damage. Do not keep conditioning a dead site because the map looks balanced.
