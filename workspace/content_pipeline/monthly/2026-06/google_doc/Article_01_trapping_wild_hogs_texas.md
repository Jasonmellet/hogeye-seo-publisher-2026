# Trapping Wild Hogs in Texas: Regulations, Permits, and What Landowners Need to Know

Texas carries one of the largest feral hog populations in the country. If you own or lease land here, feral hogs in Texas are not a hypothetical problem. They are a recurring line item in fence repairs, feed loss, and lost sleep. The first question is usually legal before it is technical.

Before you buy panels, set bait, or mount a trap camera, you need a clear picture of what Texas generally allows on **your** land. Get written permission if you do not hold the deed. Confirm transport and disposal rules before your first catch. Document current [Texas Parks and Wildlife Department (TPWD)](https://tpwd.texas.gov/) guidance with a dated screenshot. **This is not legal advice.** Rules change, and county or lease terms can add restrictions.

Once the rules are clear, the operational question is whether your **trap site, conditioning, and closure timing** are tight enough to remove whole sounders instead of educating them. Remote monitoring helps you close when the full group is inside without wasted 2 a.m. drives, but it does not replace knowing what you are allowed to do on that ground.

## Why Texas Is Ground Zero for Feral Hog Control

Feral hogs reproduce quickly, travel in sounders, and cause damage year-round. Texas landowners have invested heavily in trapping, hunting, and aerial control because the economic impact is real. That pressure is why the legal question matters early. If you are cleared to trap, the goal is not to catch one or two hogs. It is to build a trap site, condition the sounder, and close only when the full group is inside.

Texas private-land hog control rules are often more flexible than landowners expect, but that does not mean anything goes. Verify method, permission, and transport rules before you invest in panels and a trap camera.

---

## Private Land vs Public Land: Different Rules Apply

**Private land** is where most Texas trap programs run, whether the operator owns the ground or has written landowner permission. Control methods, seasons, and licensing requirements differ from **public land**, WMAs, and certain municipal areas.

On private property, sort out these items **before** you invest in trap hardware or cellular monitoring:

- Whether you are **trapping**, **hunting**, or using **contracted control**
- Whether your lease restricts methods, night work, or camera-triggered closure
- How you will **transport, dispatch, or dispose** of animals after closure (local rules and biosecurity matter)
- Whether a neighbor boundary or road frontage limits where panels can sit

Getting permission and transport rules wrong does not just create legal risk. It wastes setup time on a trap site you may have to pull down, and it disrupts conditioning if you pause mid-cycle to fix paperwork.

If you do not control the deed, get written permission before setting traps or running a camera system.

## Trapping vs Hunting: What Landowners Typically Need to Know

**Hunting** and **trapping** are different workflows with different equipment, timing, and pressure on the sounder.

**Trapping** is usually the better fit when you want to remove **multiple animals from one sounder** without repeated trips at night. It pairs well with conditioning, pre-bait, and **remote monitoring** so you trigger the gate when the full group is inside.

**Hunting** can reduce numbers but often scatters or educates the rest of the sounder. Pressure from hunting can push hogs nocturnal and trap-shy on the same property where you are trying to condition a bait site. Many Texas programs use hunting as a supplement, not the sole strategy.

## Permits, Depredation Programs, and When to Call TPWD

Texas policy has evolved over time. Landowners should verify current TPWD guidance for:

- **Landowner rights** to control feral hogs on private property
- Any **license or permit** requirements that apply to your situation (hunting vs trapping vs aerial)
- **Transport and holding** rules if you move live hogs or sell to a buyer
- **Depredation or cooperative control** programs in your county

Do not rely on forum posts or old PDFs. Pull the current TPWD feral hog resource page before you:

- Order commercial trap panels or a remote trigger system
- Commit a contractor to aerial or night shooting
- Plan live transport to a buyer or processing facility

Clear permit, transport, and holding questions **before** conditioning begins. If you pause mid-cycle to fix paperwork after the sounder is patterned, you lose closure timing and often educate the group you were about to catch.

Document what you verified and when. That saves you from rebuilding a trap program because a method you assumed was legal on your lease is not.

## Best Practices Once You Are Cleared to Trap

1. **Pick the right trap type** for your sounder size and terrain ([comparison guide](https://hogeyecameras.com/hog-trap-comparison-corral-box-drop-net/)).
2. **Place the trap where hogs are already moving** (fresh trails, rooting, or wallows), not where you wish they would walk. See our [placement guide](https://hogeyecameras.com/hog-trap-placement-guide/).
3. **Pre-bait and condition** before you expect a capture. Rushing this step causes the failures listed in [common hog trap mistakes](https://hogeyecameras.com/common-hog-trap-mistakes/).
4. **Use live video** to verify the sounder count before closure on corral and net systems.
5. **Log catches** and adjust sites. One trap in one corner rarely solves a property-wide problem.

## How Remote Monitoring Improves Closure Timing

Electronic and cellular trap systems do not change Texas law. They change **your ability to close when the full sounder is inside** without driving to the site at 2 a.m.

A remote trigger workflow looks like this:

- Camera confirms hogs are inside and the gate path is clear
- You trigger the gate from your phone or a trusted operator does
- You confirm closure on video before dispatch or handling

That reduces wasted trips and lowers the odds of closing on a partial sounder. Monitoring can help you document activity and respond faster, but it does not replace any inspection, removal, transport, or disposal requirement that applies to your property.

## FAQ

**Do I need a license to trap hogs on my own land in Texas?**  
Rules depend on method, property type, and current TPWD guidance. Pull the current TPWD feral hog page, note what applies to your situation, and call TPWD or your county extension office if anything is unclear before you set hardware.

**Is night hunting for hogs allowed in Texas?**  
Rules vary by method and property type. Confirm current regulations rather than assuming 24/7 access.

**Can I trap hogs and sell them?**  
Transport and sale rules apply. Check TPWD and any buyer requirements before you move live animals off the property.

**What should I do first if hogs just showed up?**  
Confirm damage (see our [wild hog damage field guide](https://hogeyecameras.com/wild-hog-damage-field-guide/)), verify what is allowed on your land, estimate cost impact, then choose trap type and placement.

**Does a camera-triggered trap change what I need to confirm with TPWD?**  
The trap and closure method still have to fit current rules for your property type. Confirm permissions, transport, and disposal before you install hardware, not after the first catch.
